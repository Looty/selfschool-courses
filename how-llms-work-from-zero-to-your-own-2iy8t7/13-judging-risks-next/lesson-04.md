After this capstone you will have trained a model about seven times the size of lab 8, sampled it at three temperatures, and written an honest assessment of what it can and cannot do.

## Shortcut: the ready-made scripts

Use the folder from lab 8 (`minigpt.py`, `input.txt`) and add `lab13-capstone.py` from this lesson's Resources:

```bash
python lab13-capstone.py                 # defaults: 4 layers, 128-number vectors, 3000 steps
python lab13-capstone.py 4 128 3000      # layers, vector size, steps: change one at a time
```

It was run while writing this course. The default run took about **7.5 minutes** on a laptop CPU, so start it and read on. It writes `capstone-report.txt` with your numbers and samples.

## What the run produces

The test run trained a model with **826,433 parameters** (lab 8's had 112,577; the context window doubled to 128 characters) and finished with a held-out loss of **1.545**, against 1.93 for lab 8 and 2.49 for the bigram. It then sampled 250 characters after the prompt `ROMEO:` at temperatures 0.5, 0.8 and 1.2. In the test run:

- **0.5** gave tidy, repetitive lines with real words ("The prince of the blood of my brother in...").
- **0.8** gave more varied text with invented names and some broken grammar.
- **1.2** gave text with more invented words and broken punctuation.

This is the same pattern as lesson 25: low temperature is safer and duller, high is more inventive and more broken. Your samples will differ, because training is random.

## Your assessment

Write one page (a text file next to your report) answering these questions in plain words, using your own numbers:

1. **What does the model do well?** (Cite two specific features of the sample text, such as speaker names and line breaks.)
2. **What does it do badly?** (For example: it never stays on a topic for more than a line; it invents words.)
3. **Where does it sit on the scaling story?** Compare your loss with lab 8's (1.93) and the bigram's (2.49). Which of the three axes from lesson 39, model size, data or compute, did you change? [2]
4. **What would you change next, and why?** Use Chinchilla's advice that data should grow with the model (lesson 40) to say whether your next step is more steps, more data, or a bigger model. [8]
5. **What would you refuse to use it for?** Link your answer to lesson 59.

:::low-confidence
Timings and losses depend on your CPU and library versions; the figures above come from one run. The model is a character-level toy: it demonstrates the method, not the quality of a real LLM. nanoGPT's own README, from which the Shakespeare example is borrowed, now describes itself as old and deprecated and points to nanochat. [17][18]
:::

:::key-takeaways
- A bigger mini-GPT (826,433 parameters, 3000 steps) reached held-out loss 1.545, better than lab 8 (1.93) and the bigram (2.49).
- Temperature trades tidiness for variety, as in lesson 25.
- Judging your own model honestly, with numbers and failures, is the skill that transfers to real LLMs.
:::

:::glossary
- **Capstone**: a final project that brings together what the course taught.
- **Held-out loss**: loss on text the model did not train on.
- **Context window (128)**: how many characters the model sees at once in this run.
- **Sample**: text generated by the model.
- **Report**: your numbers and samples, saved for comparison between runs.
- **Deprecated**: officially replaced; kept for reference only.
:::
